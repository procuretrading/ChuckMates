What Are the Benefits of Keeping Your Tires Properly Inflated?

Home » Blog » What Are the Benefits of Keeping Your Tires Properly Inflated?

Properly inflated tires use less fuel, help the vehicle steer and stop as it should, last longer, and carry their load without building up excess heat. The U.S. Department of Energy says correct pressure improves gas mileage by 0.6% on average, and NHTSA says it can extend a tire’s average life by 4,700 miles.

How much fuel does proper tire pressure save?

A little per tank, and it adds up. According to the U.S. Department of Energy’s fueleconomy.gov, "You can improve your gas mileage by 0.6% on average—up to 3% in some cases—by keeping your tires inflated to the proper pressure."

The same page says "Under-inflated tires can lower gas mileage by about 0.2% for every 1 psi drop in the average pressure of all tires." So the cost grows with how far low you are. A set of tires that has slowly leaked down over a season costs more than one that’s a pound off.

The DOE estimate assumes a vehicle averaging 10% under-inflation across all tires (25% in its worst case), and it converts the average benefit to $0.03 per gallon. NHTSA’s TireWise page puts the upside higher: "Properly inflating your tires can save you as much as 11 cents per gallon on fuel." The two numbers don’t contradict each other. One is an average and the other is a ceiling. Either way, it’s money back for a few minutes with a gauge.

What does each benefit look like, according to the sources?

Here is what the government sources say, in their own words:

Does tire pressure affect handling and braking?

Yes. NHTSA’s tire safety brochure, Tire Safety: Everything Rides On It, says "Properly maintained tires improve the steering, stopping, traction, and load-carrying capability of your vehicle." The placard pressure is the number the vehicle manufacturer specified for your vehicle, so that’s the pressure to hold.

Low air is also a failure risk. NHTSA’s TireWise page lists not having enough air in your tires among the maintenance lapses that can lead to a flat, a blowout, or the tread coming off, and it reports that "In 2024, a total of 511 people died on the road in tire-related crashes."

Do properly inflated tires last longer?

Yes. NHTSA says "properly inflated tires can extend the average life of a tire by 4,700 miles," and fueleconomy.gov says "Properly inflated tires are safer and last longer."

Wear isn’t only about tread depth. Among the reasons NHTSA gives to stop using a tire is "signs of irregular wear or other damage due to underinflation or overloading." A tire that spends its life low can come off early even when there’s tread left in the middle.

Why does low pressure matter more when you’re loaded or towing?

Because air pressure is what gives a tire its load-carrying capacity. NHTSA’s brochure puts it plainly: "Tire inflation pressure is the level of air in the tire that provides it with load-carrying capacity and affects the overall performance of the vehicle."

It also warns that "Excessive speed, underinflation or excessive loading, either separately or in combination, can cause heat build-up and possible tire failure," and that "Underinflated tires and overloaded vehicles are a major cause of tire failure." A full bed, highway speed, and a tire a few pounds low is the combination to avoid.

Before towing or hauling, check the placard and owner’s manual for loaded pressures. On duallys and RVs, follow the vehicle and tire manufacturer’s load tables, not a passenger-car rule of thumb.

What does "properly inflated" actually mean?

It means the cold pressure listed on your vehicle’s Tire and Loading Information placard, usually on the driver’s door jamb, or in the owner’s manual. As fueleconomy.gov puts it, "Do not use the maximum pressure printed on the tire’s sidewall." That number is the tire’s limit, not a target. Our door placard vs sidewall guide covers the difference.

Check cold, which NHTSA defines as the car not having been driven "for at least three hours," and check all tires, including the spare, at least once a month. See should you check tire pressure hot or cold for why a warm reading runs high.

Two habits that don’t work:

  • Eyeballing it. NHTSA notes that "With radial tires, it is usually not possible to determine underinflation by visual inspection."
  • Waiting for the dash light. NHTSA says a tire pressure monitoring system (TPMS) will "only activate a warning when a tire is significantly underinflated."

FAQ

Does tire pressure really affect gas mileage?

Yes, modestly. Per fueleconomy.gov, under-inflated tires can lower gas mileage by about 0.2% for every 1 psi drop in the average pressure of all tires.

Is it better to run a few extra PSI?

No. Use the cold pressure on your door placard or in the owner’s manual. The sidewall maximum is a tire limit, not a target.

How often should I check tire pressure?

At least once a month, with the tires cold, including the spare. NHTSA counts tires as cold when the car hasn’t been driven for at least three hours.

Can I tell a tire is low just by looking at it?

Usually not. NHTSA says underinflation is usually not visible on radial tires, so use a gauge.

Will my TPMS warn me before pressure gets low?

Only once a tire is significantly underinflated. A monthly gauge check catches the smaller drops that still cost fuel and tread.
